# Strategy Science Introduction and Contribution Standards

## Contents

- The SS introduction structure (5-7 paragraphs)
- Contribution language calibration
- Where SS introductions differ from JBR/SMJ
- Anti-patterns to flag in POLISH or AUDIT
- A worked rewrite (synthetic example)


The single most common reason SS desk-rejects empirical work that would clear SMJ or JBR is **weak contribution framing**. SS expects a *theoretical movement*, not a list of claims. This file describes what that movement looks like and how to write the introduction to earn one.

---

## The SS introduction structure (5-7 paragraphs)

Studied across the 4 exemplars, an SS introduction follows this arc:

### ¶1 — Theoretical or strategic problem (anchored, not gap-based)

Open with a substantive claim about the phenomenon and its strategic importance. NOT "few studies have examined..." NOT "little is known about..." Instead, anchor in:

- An accepted theoretical claim with anchor citation cluster: "Strategic foresight is the ability of managers to predict how their actions will create a competitive advantage (Barney 1986, Ahuja et al. 2005, Gavetti and Menon 2016, Csaszar and Laureiro-Martinez 2018)." (Asghar)
- A phenomenon-importance claim with citation: "A large and growing share of economic activity takes place in innovation ecosystems, industry structures in which complementary components are produced without hierarchical governance (Adner and Kapoor 2010, Jacobides et al. 2018, Ganco et al. 2020)." (Clough)
- A practitioner-relevance claim grounded in theory: "Because of rapid environmental changes, managers must often make strategic decisions under time constraints in order to seize opportunities on time and increase firm performance (Eisenhardt 1989, Bettis and Hitt 1995, Baum and Wally 2003, Csaszar and Levinthal 2016)." (Kanis)
- A field-evolution claim with citation: "In recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has sparked growing interest in the role and potential of predictive capabilities in strategic decision making." (Qu)

The opening paragraph names the primary lineage in its citation cluster.

### ¶2 — Theoretical tension or unresolved question

State what is *unresolved* within the primary lineage. This is where the contribution will live. Examples:

- "While studies outline numerous antecedents of managers' strategic foresight (Gary and Wood 2011, Helfat and Martin 2014, Helfat and Peteraf 2015, Csaszar and Laureiro-Martínez 2018, Peterson and Wu 2021, Kapoor and Wilde 2022), we suggest an important but under-researched antecedent of foresight is managers' *breadth of knowledge* gained through career experience." (Asghar — under-researched antecedent)
- "Existing strategy research lacks a framework for explaining why different ecosystem architectures arise in different contexts." (Clough — missing framework)
- "It is unclear whether predictions confer meaningful benefits and systematically inform managerial actions despite their potential to fill in 'missing information' (Agrawal et al. 2018, p. 24)." (Qu — open empirical question with theoretical implications)
- "Time constraints and large language models (LLMs) are increasingly salient factors shaping this process. We study how both jointly affect mental representations and strategic foresight..." (Kanis — joint effect of two emerging factors)

### ¶3 — Research question

Make the RQ explicit. SS RQs are usually phrased as "how do X dimensions affect Y" or "what is the role of X in shaping Y" or "under what conditions does X yield Y." Examples:

- "Accordingly, we investigate how different dimensions of managers' knowledge breadth (across industries, firms, and functions) are associated with heterogeneity in their foresight." (Asghar)
- "How do managers form predictions in different strategic contexts, including those involving key outcomes, such as market reactions related to acquisitions?" (Qu, paraphrased)
- "We investigate how time constraints and LLM use affect mental representations and strategic foresight." (Kanis)
- "In this paper, I argue that ecosystem architectures can align with market contexts." (Clough — RQ folded into theoretical claim)

### ¶4 — Empirical or analytical approach

Sketch the method and its setting-fit rationale. SS introductions justify the setting before describing the design:

- "Indeed, managers' stock purchase decisions represent a 'fruit fly' setting to study how managers' knowledge breadth affects their foresight." (Asghar)
- "We employ a 2 (time constraints: no versus yes) × 2 (LLM use: no versus yes) between-participants design..." (Kanis)
- "We compiled a data set of 13,237 acquisition transactions by U.S. public acquirers between 1976 and 2022. Next, we trained ML models..." (Qu)
- For theory papers (Clough): "I integrate Williamson's foundational work on hybrid governance arrangements with the game-theoretic approach to technical coordination..."

### ¶5 — Headline findings (empirical) or framework summary (theory)

State the main results or main framework outputs. For empirical work, give a 1-2 paragraph summary including unexpected nulls or moderators.

For Kanis: "We find that both time constraints and LLM use significantly alter mental representations but not strategic foresight." (Honest reporting of null on main DV, while highlighting representational effects.)

For Qu: "First, consistent with the theory-based view, we find that predicted market reactions to actual transactions are higher in value than those associated with random transactions." (Lead finding.)

For Clough: "First, the theory identifies an architectural trilemma... Second, the theory produces a map that predicts which economic architectures are aligned with which configurations of environmental parameters."

### ¶6 — Theoretical contribution (the critical paragraph)

This is where SS papers earn or lose acceptance. The pattern is **one or two specific theoretical movements, not a list of three vague claims**.

Acceptable movements:

| Movement type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism specification** | "We specify the mechanism by which X affects Y" | "We explicate how knowledge breadth dimensions heterogeneously affect cognitive structure and, in turn, foresight." (Asghar) |
| **Boundary refinement** | "We identify conditions under which X holds" | "We show that LLM use changes representational characteristics but does not improve foresight, suggesting that representational shifts do not translate to outcome improvements under time constraints." (Kanis) |
| **Integration** | "We integrate X and Y to show Z" | "I integrate Williamson's foundational work on hybrid governance arrangements with the game-theoretic approach to technical coordination..." (Clough) |
| **Reconciliation** | "We reconcile competing findings of X and Y by..." | (Possible: Kanis reconciles "LLMs help" vs. "LLMs harm" by showing differential effects on breadth vs. depth) |
| **Extension** | "We extend X to domain Y, with implications for Z" | "Our paper extends prior research that emphasizes backward-looking aspiration-based mechanisms (Cyert and March 1963) by highlighting a complementary forward-looking mechanism..." (Qu) |
| **New construct** | "We introduce the construct of X, distinct from Y" | "We propose that knowledge breadth, rather than being a monolithic concept, is composed of different human capital dimensions that heterogeneously affect..." (Asghar — sub-construct decomposition) |
| **New framework** | "We develop the [name] framework, which..." | "The discriminating alignment framework I develop produces a map that predicts which architectures are aligned with which..." (Clough) |
| **Methodological-theoretical** | "We develop a measure of X that operationalizes Y" | "We develop a novel measure of predicted market reactions that extrapolates prior reactions using ML models. Our findings highlight the informational value..." (Qu) |

**Unacceptable contribution patterns** (will trigger SS desk-reject):

- "We contribute to three streams of literature..." (vague three-item list)
- "We add to the [X] literature" (no movement)
- "We are the first to study Y" (no evidence; SS reviewers will check)
- "We test [established theory] in a new context" (extension without mechanism)
- "We provide important theoretical and practical implications" (boilerplate)

### ¶7 — Practical / policy / managerial implications teaser (optional, brief)

If included, this should be brief and tie back to the mechanism. Example: "Finally, our study has important implications for managers. By revealing how different knowledge breadth dimensions are differentially associated with foresight, our research may guide managers to develop foresight capabilities through the deliberate accumulation of specific types of experience over time..." (Asghar)

## Expanded exemplar logic from the 2024-2026 corpus

When the introduction feels topically relevant but not yet SS-grade, diagnose
which argument logic it should follow. Use `ss_expanded_exemplar_corpus_2024_2026.md`
for details.

| Manuscript situation | Strong SS logic | Exemplar pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A classic assumption is too simple | Assumption repair: name the assumption, show the behavioral or strategic limitation, then offer an operational way to use a better assumption | Csaszar and Eklund (2026) |
| A new technology affects strategic decision-making | Process mapping: define the SDM process, show why the technology changes feasibility, test or illustrate current capability, then map implications to search/representation/aggregation | Csaszar et al. (2024) |
| The paper challenges a dominant AI or cognition premise | Contrarian theory: state the prevailing claim, grant its valid domain, isolate the hidden premise, then replace it with a theory-based causal mechanism | Felin and Holweg (2024) |
| Growth and exit, buy and divest, or adjacent strategy processes are treated separately | Integrated process: show why a sequential account is misspecified, introduce the event or condition that forces simultaneity, and derive mechanism-testing moderators | Gibbs et al. (2026) |

The practical instruction for POLISH and SECTION mode is: do not merely match
the topic. Match the *logic gap*. A manuscript about AI can follow Csaszar et
al.'s capability-mapping logic or Felin and Holweg's contrarian-TBV logic; the
choice depends on its thesis, not its keyword list.

### Introduction wording patterns to reuse cautiously

- Assumption repair: "A central yet often implicit assumption..." -> "this leaves underspecified how..." -> "we develop a framework/method that..."
- Process mapping: "Given these opportunities and uncertainties..." -> "we address three questions..." -> "we connect X to search, representation, and aggregation..."
- Contrarian theory: "We grant that..." -> "however, this does not imply..." -> "the relevant distinction is..."
- Integrated reconfiguration: "existing work treats X and Y as sequential..." -> "we propose an integrated process..." -> "if the mechanism is correct, the effect should be amplified when..."

Use these as scaffolds, not copied prose. The user's own constructs, evidence,
and citations must fill the scaffold.

## Contribution language calibration

Use the verb that matches the movement and design:

| If the contribution is... | The verb should be... |
|---|---|
| A new theoretical mechanism | "we propose / we theorize / we explicate" |
| A boundary refinement (empirically tested) | "we show / we find / we identify" |
| A boundary refinement (formally derived) | "the framework predicts / the analysis yields / we derive" |
| An integration of two literatures | "we integrate / we combine / we bring together" |
| A new construct | "we introduce / we propose the construct of / we operationalize" |
| A new framework | "we develop / we build / we propose the framework of" |
| An empirical contribution with cross-sectional data | "we document / we are the first to empirically examine [with appropriate hedge] / our findings are consistent with" |
| A negative/null finding | "we find / we report / our results do not support" |

## Where SS introductions differ from JBR/SMJ

1. **More theoretical lineage anchoring in ¶1**. JBR can open with a business problem; SS opens with a theoretical claim or named lineage.
2. **More restrained contribution language**. JBR papers often claim "implications for theory and practice"; SS expects you to commit to one specific theoretical movement and engage the existing literature substantively.
3. **More tolerant of null/mixed findings** if the theoretical contribution is sharp. Kanis et al. 2026 publishes a study where the headline DV (strategic foresight) showed no significant treatment effect, but the representational measures did — and the paper's contribution is framed around the gap between representational change and outcome change.
4. **More tolerant of pure-theory papers** (Clough 2026 has no empirics). SMJ rarely publishes pure theory; AMR is the prototype outlet; SS sits between AMR and SMJ on this dimension.
5. **More tolerant of formal/computational work** when the mechanism is strategic.
6. **Less tolerant of pure-empirical "X predicts Y in a new context"** without theoretical movement.

## Anti-patterns to flag in POLISH or AUDIT

1. **"Few studies have examined..."** in ¶1 → flag as MEDIUM; rewrite to a substantive opening.
2. **No named lineage by end of ¶2** → flag as HIGH; cannot proceed without identifying the primary lineage.
3. **RQ not explicit by end of ¶3** → flag as HIGH; introduction reads as a meander.
4. **Three vague contribution claims** → flag as HIGH; rewrite as one or two specific movements.
5. **"First study to..."** without evidence → flag as HIGH; soften or remove.
6. **Practical implications dropped without grounding** → flag as MEDIUM; tie to mechanism.
7. **Empirical claims in a theory paper** (e.g., "we find" in a Clough-style paper) → flag as HIGH; rewrite to "the framework predicts" / "the analysis yields."

## A worked rewrite (synthetic example)

### BEFORE (JBR-style, mis-fit for SS)

> Few studies have examined how AI tools impact strategic decision-making. This study examines how the use of large language models affects decision quality. Using a sample of 348 participants on Prolific, we conduct an experiment manipulating time constraints and LLM use. We find that LLM use changes the breadth and depth of decision-makers' analyses. Our study makes three contributions: first, we contribute to the strategic decision-making literature; second, we contribute to the AI literature; third, we contribute to the time-pressure literature.

Issues:
- "Few studies have examined" → literature-gap opening
- No named theoretical lineage
- No theoretical tension
- "Three vague contribution items"
- Empirical claim ("we find") not anchored in mechanism
- No theoretical movement

### AFTER (SS-calibrated; modeled on Kanis et al. 2026)

> Strategic foresight — the ability to predict strategic outcomes — depends on how decision-makers represent strategic problems (Csaszar 2018, Csaszar and Laureiro-Martínez 2018). Time constraints and large language models (LLMs) are increasingly salient factors shaping this process. Building on the Carnegie tradition (Cyert and March 1963, Simon 1997) and the representational approach to strategic decision-making (Csaszar and Laureiro-Martínez 2018), we study how both jointly affect mental representations and strategic foresight in a startup evaluation task (N = 348).
>
> Using a 2 × 2 (time constraints: no versus yes) × (LLM use: no versus yes) between-participants design, we show that both time constraints and LLM use significantly alter the characteristics of mental representations. Despite these representational shifts, neither time constraints nor LLM use are found to significantly change strategic foresight. Additional analyses indicate, for instance, that LLM use increases information overload and reduces psychological ownership.
>
> Our findings can be viewed as a cautionary case for the effectiveness of LLM use in strategic decision-making and contribute to the literature on strategic foresight and representations in two specific ways. First, we extend the representational approach (Csaszar and Laureiro-Martínez 2018) by showing that representational characteristics — whether induced by time constraints or LLM use — do not necessarily translate into changes in strategic foresight. This refines the link between representations and outcomes in the lens-model tradition. Second, we contribute to the emerging literature on AI in strategic decision-making ([verified AI-strategy anchors]) by identifying boundary conditions under which LLMs do not improve strategic outcomes despite altering cognitive inputs.

Improvements:
- Opens with anchored claim and primary-lineage citation cluster
- Names theoretical tension (representational change vs. outcome change)
- Method appears with setting-fit rationale
- Honest null reporting on main DV
- Two specific contributions, each tied to a named lineage
- Empirical claim ("we show") is matched to design (between-subjects experiment)
- Hedging ("can be viewed as") is calibrated
