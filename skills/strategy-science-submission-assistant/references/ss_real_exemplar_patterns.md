## §1. Introduction openings

All four open with a substantive theoretical or phenomenon-anchored claim — NEVER a "literature gap" trope.

### Pattern A: Theory-anchored opening with citation cluster

```
Strategic foresight is the ability of managers to predict how their actions
will create a competitive advantage (Barney 1986, Ahuja et al. 2005, Gavetti
and Menon 2016, Csaszar and Laureiro-Martinez 2018). Perceptions of future
value direct how managers formulate, select, and commit to strategies they
believe have the greatest value-creating potential (Danneels 2002, Barnett
et al. 2003, Cummings and Knott 2018, Vinokurova and Kapoor 2020).
```
— Asghar et al., opening ¶1

### Pattern B: Phenomenon-anchored opening with stakeholder relevance

```
A large and growing share of economic activity takes place in innovation
ecosystems, industry structures in which complementary components are produced
without hierarchical governance (Adner and Kapoor 2010, Jacobides et al. 2018,
Ganco et al. 2020). Phenomena as diverse as digital platforms, open source
software communities, high-tech manufacturing industries, and communication
and transportation service providers are all studied under the rubric of
innovation ecosystems (Altman et al. 2022).
```
— Clough 2026, opening ¶1

### Pattern C: Practitioner-relevance opening grounded in theory

```
Because of rapid environmental changes, managers must often make strategic
decisions under time constraints in order to seize opportunities on time and
increase firm performance (Eisenhardt 1989, Bettis and Hitt 1995, Baum and
Wally 2003, Csaszar and Levinthal 2016). Time constraints present managers
with the challenge of rapidly developing mental representations (i.e.,
simplified models of reality) of strategic problems and of predicting the
outcomes of alternative options (Gavetti and Menon 2016, Csaszar and
Laureiro-Martínez 2018).
```
— Kanis et al. 2026, opening ¶1

### Pattern D: Field-evolution opening

```
In recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has sparked growing
interest in the role and potential of predictive capabilities in strategic
decision making. One area of strategic decision making in which predictive
capabilities have drawn widespread interest is acquisitions. The increasing
prominence of predictive analytics in this realm raises several significant
questions, including what role predictions play in acquisition decision
making and what informational benefits they confer across various stages
of the acquisition process.
```
— Qu et al. 2026, opening ¶1

## §2. Research question phrasing

### Pattern: Explicit "how" / "what role" / "under what conditions"

- "Accordingly, we investigate how different dimensions of managers' knowledge breadth (across industries, firms, and functions) are associated with heterogeneity in their foresight." (Asghar)
- "We investigate how time constraints and LLM use affect mental representations and strategic foresight." (Kanis, paraphrased)
- "We examine the role of predictions in acquisition decision making..." (Qu)
- "In this paper, I argue that ecosystem architectures can align with market contexts." (Clough — RQ embedded as a claim)

## §3. Contribution paragraph patterns

### Pattern: Two specific theoretical movements, not three vague claims

```
Our paper offers primary contributions to two literatures. First, we extend
the literature on foresight. This work shows how an individual's cognition
influences their ability to predict the impact of managerial actions on firm
outcomes (Gary and Wood 2011, Csaszar and Laureiro-Martínez 2018). Further,
while some managers may improve their foresight by updating their beliefs
(Kapoor and Wilde 2022), others may have cognitive difficulty in adapting
their mental models (Barr et al. 1992, Tripsas and Gavetti 2000). Our study
adds to this body of work by highlighting how the type of career experience
that shapes human capital may affect managers' foresight.

Second, we build on the strategic human capital literature that outlines how
managerial human capital attributes shape firm-level outcomes (e.g., Castanias
and Helfat 2001, Chen et al. 2021a). By explicating how knowledge breadth
is composed of different dimensions that heterogeneously shape managers'
decisions and capabilities, sometimes in opposite ways, we extend prior work
that has focused mostly on one human capital dimension (e.g., Mannucci and
Yong 2018) or conceptualized knowledge breadth as a monolithic, aggregated
construct (e.g., Custódio et al. 2013, 2019).
```
— Asghar et al.

### Pattern: Cautionary findings with explicit re-framing

```
Our findings can be viewed as a cautionary case for the effectiveness of LLM
use in strategic decision-making. Thus, our findings suggest several avenues
for future research on LLM use and strategic foresight, particularly regarding
the interplay between individual cognitive processes and the contextual
factors of strategic decisions.
```
— Kanis et al. abstract (the paper's null results on the main DV are converted into a theoretically meaningful contribution about boundary conditions)

### Pattern: Theoretical movement via methodological innovation

```
We provide evidence supporting these arguments by developing a novel measure
of predicted market reactions that extrapolates prior market reactions using
machine learning models. Our findings highlight the informational value that
predictions confer in acquisition decision making and provide formal support
for investing in predictive capabilities and AI in such contexts.
```
— Qu et al. abstract

### Pattern: Integration of two literatures producing a new framework

```
By integrating Williamson's foundational work on hybrid governance arrangements
with the game-theoretic approach to technical coordination, I uncover a
coordination trade-off, between speed and scope, and an architectural trilemma
in which a given architecture can provide at most two out of three adaptation
attributes. The discriminating alignment framework I develop produces a map
that predicts which architectures are aligned with which configurations of
environmental parameters.
```
— Clough 2026 abstract

## §4. Hypothesis labeling

```
**Hypothesis 1a (H1a).** *Managers with greater industry knowledge breadth
are associated with less foresight regarding the performance of product
introductions than those who have narrower industry knowledge breadth.*
```
— Asghar et al.

```
**Hypothesis 1a.** *Time constraints reduce the breadth of mental
representations.*

**Hypothesis 1b.** *Time constraints do not influence the depth of mental
representations.*

**Hypothesis 1c.** *Time constraints increase the consensus of mental
representations.*

**Hypothesis 1d.** *Time constraints decrease strategic foresight accuracy.*
```
— Kanis et al.

```
**Hypothesis 1.** *Predictions of market reactions based on historical
transactions and their incoming cues confer informational value and foresight
and enhance acquisition decision making in ex ante deal selection.*

**Hypothesis 2.** *The larger the absolute disparity between actual and
predicted market reactions related to an acquisition announcement, the longer
the deal completion time.*
```
— Qu et al.

### Pattern observations:
- Bold "Hypothesis 1a (H1a)." or "Hypothesis 1." with period after
- Italicized statement
- Full sentence with period at end
- Indented or block style
- For multi-part hypotheses, use sub-letters (1a, 1b, 1c, 1d)

## §5. Method section openings

### Archival panel
```
We tested our hypotheses using a sample of public companies listed in the
S&P 500 that made product introduction announcements between 2016 and 2018.
We sourced product introduction data from the Capital IQ Key Developments
database, which provides summaries of firms' news and events. We merged our
product introduction announcement data with data on insider trading
transactions...
```
— Asghar et al., Section 3 opening

### Experiment
```
We adapted an established task in the field of strategic foresight — namely,
evaluation of two startups based on pitch videos and selection of the more
successful startup (Csaszar and Laureiro-Martínez 2018, Heshmati and Csaszar
2023). In particular, we adapted this task in two ways: (1) We introduced
time constraints to some participants, and (2) we provided some participants
with an LLM chatbot.
```
— Kanis et al., Methods → Task opening

### Archival + ML
```
To test our hypotheses regarding the effects of predictions on acquisition
decision making, we constructed a sample comprising all completed acquisition
transactions by U.S. public acquirers in which the acquirer obtained 100%
ownership of the target firm. The data were obtained from the Refinitiv
database and span the period from 1976 (the earliest year available) through
2022.
```
— Qu et al., Section 3.1 opening

## §6. Discussion section openings

### Pattern: One-paragraph overview before theoretical implications

```
We investigated the role of predictions in acquisition decision making using
a set of propositions grounded in behavioral decision-making theory. As
interest in predictive analytics continues to grow, particularly with the
rapid diffusion of AI, it is increasingly important for strategy scholars
to develop a better understanding of how predictions shape strategic decision
processes.
```
— Qu et al., Section 5.1 opening

### Pattern: Discussion engages the lineage's open questions

```
Our study contributes to the literature on strategic foresight, the role of
LLMs in strategic decision-making ([verified AI-strategy anchors])
in general, and the use of LLMs in strategic decision-making in particular
([verified AI-strategy anchors]).

First, our findings provide empirical evidence on how context factors —
specifically, time constraints — can shape the structure of mental
representations underlying strategic foresight. Whereas prior work has
theorized that time constraints may lead to different mental representations
(Csaszar and Levinthal 2016, Csaszar 2018), we show that they selectively
reduce breadth without significantly affecting depth.
```
— Kanis et al., Discussion → Theoretical Implications

## §8. Limitations & future research patterns

### Pattern: Numbered, substantive limitations (Kanis pattern, 7 specific limitations)

```
Limitations and Future Research

As with all studies, our work has limitations that open avenues for further
research. First, our use of Kickstarter startup evaluations as the strategic
decision-making context may raise questions of generalizability. However,
this design has proven useful in various contexts...

Second, our sample relied on participants from Prolific...

Third, and relatedly, relying on an online participant pool bears the risk
that our participants differed in motivation compared with participants used
in prior research...

Fourth, our study focuses on individuals without considering group-level
processes or multiagent interaction...

Fifth, we excluded a relatively large share of participants from our analyses...

Sixth, to classify participants' pros and cons, we relied on LLM-based coding.
This approach could raise the concern that the coding process introduced bias...

Seventh, we measured strategic foresight at only one point in time.
```
— Kanis et al.

### Pattern observations:
- Numbered list (not bullets)
- Each limitation is concrete (not boilerplate)
- Each limitation triggers a future research direction
- Robust handling: e.g., the high exclusion rate is acknowledged AND robustness-checked

## §9. Invariants (the things that ALWAYS hold across SS papers)

These are non-negotiable patterns. Use them as desk-reject triggers if absent.

1. **Primary theoretical lineage named in introduction** with anchor citation cluster
2. **INFORMS citation style**: (Author year) without comma, "(Author and Author year)" with "and" not "&", "(Author et al. year)" for 3+
3. **Hypothesis labeling in bold-italic** with period after the label
4. **Standard errors in parentheses** in regression tables, NOT t-statistics
5. **Section structure** follows the 1-Introduction → 2-Theory → 3-Methods → 4-Results → 5-Discussion pattern (or pure-theory variant)
6. **Discussion subsections**: Overview / Theoretical Implications / Practical (or Managerial) Implications / Limitations and Future Research
7. **References in INFORMS style**: surname + initials, journal abbreviation, volume(issue):page-page
8. **Acknowledgments** thank editor by name, reviewers, conference audiences, specific colleagues
9. **IRB approval cited** for human-subjects work
10. **Pre-registration cited** when applicable
11. **AI-use transparency** for LLM-assisted writing or measurement where requested or methodologically necessary
12. **Notes** for substantive asides appear in some published articles, but the official submission guide says endnotes and footnotes should be avoided
13. **Two specific theoretical movements** (not three vague claims) in the contribution paragraph
14. **Theory-paper voice**: "we propose / we argue / the framework predicts" for pure-theory papers; "we find / we show / our results indicate" for empirical papers — never mix
15. **Settings argued to be informative** for the theoretical mechanism ("fruit fly" arguments, established-task adaptations, scale-of-data arguments)

If any of these invariants is missing, the manuscript likely fails the SS norm in that dimension. Flag and address.

## §10. Anti-patterns (things SS papers DO NOT do)

1. Open with "Few studies have examined..." or "Little is known about..."
2. Claim "first study to..." without evidence
3. List three vague contributions in the introduction
4. Use causal verbs with cross-sectional designs
5. Anthropomorphize LLMs ("the LLM understood...")
6. Embed practical implications without grounding in mechanism
7. Include generic limitations boilerplate ("single context, single time-point")
8. Use comma between author and year in citations (`(Csaszar, 2018)` is wrong; `(Csaszar 2018)` is right)
9. Use bullet points in main text
10. Use empirical verbs in pure-theory papers
11. Skip the pre-registration disclosure when one exists
12. Omit human-coder benchmarks for LLM-coded measures
13. Skip the manipulation check for experiments
14. Use a single LLM without sensitivity to alternatives
15. Skip the IRB statement for human-subjects work
