# Contribution Statement Examples

JBR reviewers and editors consistently reject vague contribution language. This file gives:

1. Six canonical **contribution types** (mechanism, boundary, integration, reconciliation, contextualization, clarification)
2. For each type: a **BEFORE** (vague) and **AFTER** (specific) paragraph
3. **ANNOTATION** explaining what changed

These are illustrative paragraphs. Do not copy the substantive content; copy the **structural moves**.

---

## Contribution type taxonomy (six types)

| Type | What it does | Verb pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Explains *how* / *why* an established relation holds | "We specify the [mechanism] through which X relates to Y" |
| Boundary | Identifies *when* / *for whom* a relation holds | "We show that the X–Y relation depends on [boundary condition]" |
| Integration | Joins two theories to explain a multi-path phenomenon | "By integrating [theory A] and [theory B], we account for both [path 1] and [path 2]" |
| Reconciliation | Resolves contradictory empirical findings | "We reconcile mixed evidence on [relation] by showing that [resolving factor] explains the variation" |
| Contextualization | Adapts a theory to a new context, altering its core assumptions | "We contextualize [theory] within [context], showing how [contextual feature] alters [theoretical assumption]" |
| Clarification | Disentangles previously conflated constructs | "We disentangle [construct A] from [construct B], showing that only [construct A] [verb] [outcome]" |

## Example 1 — Mechanism contribution

### BEFORE (generic; the four-bullet list of vague claims that JBR reviewers hate)

> This study makes several important contributions. First, it enriches the literature on digital transformation by showing that AI matters for firm performance. Second, it contributes to research on managerial cognition by linking AI to executive attention. Third, it extends the attention-based view. Fourth, it offers important implications for managers and policymakers about the role of AI in firms.

### AFTER (mechanism-specific; one tight paragraph)

> This study contributes to the attention-based view (Ocasio, 1997) by specifying an *attention-infrastructure* mechanism that links AI capability building to managers' temporal orientation. Prior work has documented that AI affects firm outcomes, but the cognitive pathway has remained a black box. We argue and show that discovery-oriented AI investments reshape three pillars of executive attention — situated attention, focus of attention, and the structural distribution of attention — and that this reshaping is associated with longer planning horizons in top management. The contribution is not that AI affects performance, but that AI affects performance *through* a measurable attentional pathway that can be designed and managed.

### ANNOTATION

| What changed | Why |
|---|---|
| Four vague bullets → one focused paragraph | JBR rewards one specific theoretical movement, not a list of "enriches" claims |
| Named the theoretical conversation (Ocasio, 1997 ABV) | Reviewers need to know **which** literature you contribute to |
| Named the specific mechanism (attention infrastructure with three pillars) | The mechanism *is* the contribution; without it, the paper just adds another correlate |
| Reframed the "what's new" as "*through* a measurable attentional pathway" | Differentiates from prior AI-performance papers |
| Removed "important implications for managers and policymakers" | Generic; replaced by tying the contribution to a designable/manageable pathway |

## Example 2 — Boundary condition contribution

### BEFORE (generic; "extends X by showing Y matters")

> This study extends the dynamic capabilities view by showing that dynamic capabilities have an effect on firm performance. We also find that environmental dynamism matters for this relationship. Our findings contribute to the strategic management literature.

### AFTER (boundary-specific; tells the reader **when** the relation holds)

> This study extends the dynamic capabilities view (Teece, 2007) by specifying the boundary conditions under which sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring routines translate into performance gains. Prior empirical work treats dynamic capabilities as universally beneficial. We show that the capability-to-performance translation is *conditional*: it strengthens under high environmental dynamism — where the capability is structurally aligned with the demand for change — and weakens under low dynamism, where the same routines become slack-absorbing. The contribution is theoretical (a structural-fit boundary specification) rather than descriptive (one more positive coefficient for dynamic capabilities).

### ANNOTATION

| What changed | Why |
|---|---|
| Named the theory + canonical citation (Teece, 2007) | Anchors the conversation |
| Specified the boundary direction (stronger under high dynamism, weaker under low) | Not just "moderates" — names the substantive contingency |
| Named the **theoretical** mechanism for the boundary (structural fit) | Boundary conditions need a theory, not just a moderator |
| Closed with "theoretical (a structural-fit boundary specification) rather than descriptive" | Pre-empts the reviewer comment "you only added a moderator" |

## Example 3 — Integration contribution

### BEFORE (generic; "combines X and Y theories")

> Our study integrates resource-based view, dynamic capabilities, and institutional theory to study firm internationalization. We contribute to international business research by combining these perspectives.

### AFTER (integration with division of labor between theories)

> This study integrates the resource-based view and institutional theory to explain why internationalizing firms face *both* a resource-leverage path (firm-internal capabilities translating into foreign-market entry) and an institutional-distance path (host-country institutional alignment determining entry success). Prior work has treated these paths as alternative explanations, generating an unresolved debate over whether internationalization is "resource-driven" or "institution-driven." We show that the two paths are complementary, not competing: resource leverage explains *which* firms can attempt entry, while institutional distance explains *whether* entry succeeds once attempted. The contribution is a division-of-labor integration that resolves the debate rather than running two regressions side by side.

### ANNOTATION

| What changed | Why |
|---|---|
| Cut "and institutional theory to combine perspectives" — kept two theories | Three-theory integrations rarely work; two is the limit for clean argument |
| Specified the role of each theory ("resource leverage explains which firms can attempt entry, institutional distance explains whether entry succeeds") | Division of labor is what makes integration substantive |
| Named the prior debate the integration resolves | Integration contributions need a *problem* they integrate to solve |
| Closed with "rather than running two regressions side by side" | Pre-empts the "you just added a second predictor" reviewer comment |

## Example 4 — Reconciliation contribution

### BEFORE (generic; "reconciles mixed findings")

> Prior studies have found mixed results on the relationship between CEO compensation and firm risk-taking. Some studies find positive effects, others find negative effects. This study reconciles these mixed findings by examining the moderating role of CEO power.

### AFTER (reconciliation with named resolving factor)

> Empirical evidence on CEO equity compensation and firm risk-taking is contradictory: 12 of 28 studies in a recent meta-analysis report positive associations and 9 report negative associations, with the remainder null. We argue that the apparent contradiction stems from a previously unmeasured construct — *the temporal structure of equity vesting*. When vesting is concentrated in the near term (cliff vesting within 2 years), equity functions as a short-horizon incentive and is associated with reduced strategic risk-taking. When vesting is distributed over longer horizons (graded vesting over 5+ years), equity functions as a long-horizon incentive and is associated with increased strategic risk-taking. We show that re-coding 28 prior studies by vesting structure resolves 78% of the apparent disagreement. The contribution is to substitute a measurement refinement for the prior debate's polarization.

### ANNOTATION

| What changed | Why |
|---|---|
| Quantified the contradiction (12 positive, 9 negative, others null) | Specificity gives the reconciliation traction |
| Named the resolving factor ("temporal structure of equity vesting") | The resolving factor *is* the contribution |
| Specified *how* the factor resolves the contradiction (with directional logic) | Reconciliation needs a mechanism, not just "we add a moderator" |
| Closed with a quantified resolution ("resolves 78% of the apparent disagreement") | Shows the reconciliation actually works |

## Example 5 — Contextualization contribution

### BEFORE (generic; "we study X in [country/industry]")

> This study contextualizes dynamic capabilities theory by examining it in the Chinese context. We find that dynamic capabilities affect firm performance in China.

### AFTER (contextualization with named contextual feature altering theoretical assumption)

> Dynamic capabilities theory was developed in Western settings characterized by relatively complete markets for technology and labor. We contextualize the theory to Chinese listed firms operating under a *state-permeated* market structure, in which factor markets for skilled labor and technology are partially substituted by state allocation. Under these conditions, the theory's core assumption — that sensing and seizing routines must extract resources from external markets — does not hold uniformly. Instead, sensing and seizing operate through dual channels: market-based extraction (consistent with the original theory) and state-aligned extraction (a contextual variant). We show empirically that high-performing Chinese firms operate both channels, while purely market-channel firms underperform. The contribution is to specify a state-permeation boundary on the universality of dynamic capabilities — not to confirm that the theory "also works in China."

### ANNOTATION

| What changed | Why |
|---|---|
| Named the theoretical assumption being modified (complete markets for technology and labor) | Contextualization needs to identify the assumption it alters |
| Named the contextual feature (state-permeated market structure) | "Chinese context" is too broad; the actual contextual feature matters |
| Specified the modification (sensing/seizing operate through dual channels) | The modification *is* the contribution |
| Closed with "not to confirm that the theory 'also works in China'" | Pre-empts the most common contextualization reviewer comment |

## Example 6 — Clarification contribution

### BEFORE (generic; "we study the relationship between X and Y")

> We study the relationship between innovation and firm performance. We find that innovation is positively associated with performance.

### AFTER (clarification disentangling conflated constructs)

> Prior work conflates two distinct innovation activities — *exploratory* innovation (search for technologically distant knowledge) and *exploitative* innovation (refinement of existing technological trajectories) — and reports inconsistent effects on firm performance. We disentangle the two activities using patent-citation-based measures with non-overlapping IPC class boundaries, and show that exploratory and exploitative innovation operate on different timescales: exploitative innovation is associated with short-term performance (1-year ROA), while exploratory innovation is associated with long-term performance (5-year market value) but penalized in the short term. Treating the two as a single "innovation" construct masks this temporal asymmetry. The contribution is a construct-level disentanglement that explains why "innovation–performance" studies disagree.

### ANNOTATION

| What changed | Why |
|---|---|
| Named the construct being disentangled (exploratory vs. exploitative innovation) | Clarification needs explicit construct labels |
| Named the measurement that achieves the disentanglement (patent-citation-based, non-overlapping IPC classes) | The measurement is part of the contribution |
| Specified what the disentanglement reveals (temporal asymmetry of effects) | Disentanglement without a payoff is just relabeling |
| Closed with "explains why 'innovation–performance' studies disagree" | Ties the contribution to a known empirical puzzle |

## Universal moves across all six types

When polishing a contribution paragraph:

1. **Cut the vague bullets.** Replace any "we enrich the literature on…", "we contribute to research on…", "we add to the conversation" with a specific theoretical movement.
2. **Name the theory and the seminal citation.** "We contribute to [theory] (citation)" anchors the conversation.
3. **Name what specifically you do to the theory.** Mechanism / boundary / integration / reconciliation / contextualization / clarification — pick one as primary; secondary contributions can ride along as a second sentence.
4. **Pre-empt the reviewer comment.** Each contribution type has a typical reviewer pushback (see the AFTER closures for examples).
5. **Mirror the contribution between intro and discussion.** Whatever sentence closes the intro's contribution paragraph should reappear (verbatim or near-paraphrased) in the discussion's §6.2.

If the user's paper genuinely has two contributions, structure them as **primary + secondary**, not as two parallel claims. JBR rewards a clear primary contribution.
