# JBR House Style

JBR publishes for a business-research audience that includes academics, practitioners, and policy readers. The house style is **clear, business-relevant, mechanism-explicit, and hedged-only-where-the-design-requires-it**. The style is closer to *Strategic Management Journal* or *Long Range Planning* than to *Administrative Science Quarterly* or *Academy of Management Review*: less ornate prose, more decision-relevant content.

This file is applied at Stage 6 of the polishing pipeline, **after** the argument spine, theory, and method have been stabilized. Do not apply style edits to a draft whose claim chain is still broken — fix the substance first.

---

## 1. Voice and stance

| DO | DON'T |
|---|---|
| Active voice in introduction, hypothesis development, discussion ("We argue that…", "Firms invest in AI to…") | Passive voice as default ("It is argued in this paper that…") |
| First-person plural ("We", "Our") | Third-person self-reference ("the authors") |
| Direct claims with calibrated verbs | Hedge-stacking ("might possibly potentially suggest") |
| Confident statement of contribution | Apologetic contribution ("Our modest paper attempts…") |
| Methods section may use passive (standard convention) | Methods section overusing first-person decision narratives |

## 2. Sentence-level conventions

### Length and rhythm

- Average sentence length: **18–25 words**.
- Maximum sentence length: **35 words**. Anything longer should be split.
- Vary length: a 30-word claim followed by a 10-word implication reads better than two 22-word sentences.

### Subjects

- The grammatical subject of a sentence should usually be the conceptual subject of the claim.
  - ✓ "Discovery-oriented AI capability building reduces managerial myopia."
  - ✗ "It is the case that managerial myopia is reduced by AI capability building."

### Verbs

- Prefer concrete verbs to abstract ones: *shapes, narrows, redirects, accelerates, dampens, allocates* > *impacts, influences, affects*.
- Reserve "impact" as a **noun** for substantive consequence, not as a verb stand-in for "affect."

### Definitions

- Define every focal construct on **first use**, in the running text or in a footnote.
- After first use, use the **same term** every time. Do not alternate (e.g., "AI capability," "AI competence," "AI capacity") for the same construct.

### Numerals

- Spell out numbers one through nine; numerals for 10 and above (Elsevier convention; check current guideline).
- Percentages always numerical (e.g., 7%, not "seven percent").
- Effect sizes reported with one or two decimal places, not five.

### Abbreviations and acronyms

- Spell out on first use, then abbreviate.
- Avoid more than 4–5 acronyms in a paper. If you have more, the construct space is too cluttered.
- Common JBR acronyms (no need to expand on first use): SEM, CFA, OLS, IV, RDD, DiD, FE, GLS, GMM, ANOVA, PLS.

## 3. Paragraph structure

### Topic sentences

- Every paragraph leads with a topic sentence stating the **claim of the paragraph**.
- The rest of the paragraph develops and supports that claim.
- No "stage-setting" or "next, we discuss" sentences as topic sentences.

### Paragraph length

- Target: **5–8 sentences** per paragraph.
- Maximum: **150 words**. Split anything longer.

### Internal logic

- Each paragraph should have one of these moves:
  - **Claim → evidence → implication**
  - **Phenomenon → puzzle → resolution**
  - **Theory → application → boundary**
  - **Finding → interpretation → contribution**
- Reject paragraphs that are just a stack of citations.

### Transitions

- Use logical connectors sparingly: "however," "in contrast," "by contrast," "in addition," "thus," "therefore."
- Avoid: "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally" — these usually signal that the connection is weak.
- "First / Second / Third" enumeration is fine when there are genuinely parallel points.

## 4. Citation style (rhetorical, not bibliographic)

- **Integrated citations** when the cited author's name is part of the argument:
  - "March (1991) distinguishes exploration from exploitation, defining…"
- **Parenthetical citations** when the cited work is supporting evidence:
  - "Exploration involves search, experimentation, and risk-taking (March, 1991)."
- **3–5 citations** maximum per claim in the running text. If a claim has 8+ citations, split it into specific sub-claims.
- **Don't cite by quantity** ("see, e.g., X, Y, Z, …") as a substitute for argument.
- **Avoid citation chains** ("X (2010) cited Y (1990) who cited Z (1970)"). Cite the source you actually engage with.

---

## 5. Theory section style

- **Define before use**: every construct defined before any hypothesis invokes it.
- **One theory primary**: the same theory introduced in §3.1 must close the discussion. Auxiliary theories named as auxiliary ("Building on the attention-based view (Ocasio, 1997) and drawing on [auxiliary theory] for [specific concept]…").
- **Mechanism in italics or bold** at first introduction (helps reviewers spot it).
- **Hypotheses in italics, indented**, with explicit direction:
  - *H1: Discovery-oriented AI capability building is negatively associated with managerial temporal myopia.*

---

## 6. Methods section style

- **Past tense, active or passive**, both acceptable.
- **Construct ↔ measure ↔ source** in a single table where possible (saves prose, helps reviewers).
- **Replication-sufficient detail**: if a methods-savvy reader cannot replicate within 20% effort from your description, add detail.
- **Cite measurement validation** for every borrowed scale; if you developed a new scale, report development steps in the body or an appendix.

---

## 7. Results section style

- **Effect-size-first reporting**: "A one-standard-deviation increase in X is associated with a [β coefficient × SD of X] change in Y (p < 0.01)."
- **Calibrated verb** matched to design (see `jbr_claim_evidence_matrix.md`).
- **Tables stand alone**: the table caption should let a reader understand the table without reading the text.
- **Footnote the model choice**: if you used OLS instead of GMM, say why in a footnote, not in the body.

---

## 8. Discussion section style

- **Interpret, don't restate.** If a paragraph reads like a results summary, rewrite it as an interpretation.
- **Calibrated language** carried over from results.
- **Specificity in implications**: "Managers should X in context Y because the mechanism Z that we identify suggests…" — not "Managers should consider X."
- **Limitations as design-bounded**: name the specific design feature that limits the claim, and what design would resolve it.

## 9. JBR-specific phrasing to prefer

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "Firms" | "Companies" (acceptable, but JBR norm is "firms") |
| "Managers" or "executives" | "People in management positions" |
| "Employees" or "workers" | "Human resources" (when referring to people) |
| "Performance" with qualifier (financial, innovation, market) | "Performance" undefined |
| "Innovation" with qualifier (exploratory, exploitative, radical, incremental, product, process) | "Innovation" undefined |
| "Capability" (when arguing dynamic capabilities lens) | "Competence" / "Capacity" (used loosely) |
| "Boundary condition" | "Limit" or "constraint" (in theoretical sense) |
| "Mechanism" | "Channel" (acceptable in econ; less common in JBR) |
| "Association" / "relationship" | "Correlation" (when claim is theoretical) |

## 10. Style red flags to remove

- "It is interesting to note that…" → just state the point
- "It should be mentioned that…" → just mention it
- "The aim of this paper is to…" → "We examine…" or "This study asks…"
- "In this paper, we propose that…" → "We propose that…" (cut "in this paper")
- "It has been shown that…" → cite the work that showed it
- "In recent years…" / "In today's…" → too vague; name the period
- "Increasingly important" → measure or specify the increase
- "Plays a critical role" → describe the role
- "Recent studies have shown…" → cite the recent studies
- "There is a growing literature on…" → name the conversation

## 11. Final sentence-level pass checklist

After Stage 6, run:

- [ ] No sentence > 35 words
- [ ] No paragraph > 150 words
- [ ] Every focal construct defined before first use
- [ ] Same term used for the same construct every time (no synonym drift)
- [ ] Active voice in intro and discussion; passive acceptable in method
- [ ] No "Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "Additionally" outside genuine parallel enumeration
- [ ] No "first paper to…" / "no prior work…" without supporting evidence
- [ ] Every causal-sounding verb checked against `jbr_claim_evidence_matrix.md`
- [ ] No style red flags from §10 remaining
