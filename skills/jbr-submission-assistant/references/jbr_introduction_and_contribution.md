# JBR Introduction and Contribution

Use this reference for title, abstract, introduction, theory contribution, and discussion work.

## Abstract Pattern

Keep the abstract under the current JBR word limit. A strong JBR abstract usually compresses six moves:

1. Business problem or theoretical tension.
2. Research question or explanatory aim.
3. Theoretical lens or mechanism.
4. Data, context, and method.
5. Main findings, with claim strength calibrated to design.
6. Theoretical and practical/social contribution.

Avoid:

- Generic novelty claims.
- "Few studies have examined..." as the main contribution.
- Findings that sound causal when the design is correlational.
- Methods detail that crowds out contribution.

## Introduction Pattern

Use this six-move introduction logic:

1. Phenomenon: a real business, organizational, market, or societal problem.
2. Scholarly tension: what existing work explains and what remains unresolved.
3. Theoretical insufficiency: the missing mechanism, contingency, integration, or contextualization.
4. Research question: direct and visible.
5. Study design: setting, data, method, and why this context is theoretically informative.
6. Contribution preview: precise changes to theory, practice, and/or society.

Hard checks:

- The research question should be visible within the first 1-2 pages.
- The manuscript should not rely on "little is known" or sample novelty as the main motivation.
- The introduction should preview what the paper contributes, not merely summarize the results.
- The contribution should reappear in the discussion without inflation.

## Contribution Types

Classify the manuscript's primary contribution before rewriting contribution language:

| Contribution type | What must be shown |
| --- | --- |
| Extend theory | The paper changes the theory's boundary, scope, or application conditions. |
| Integrate theories | Two or more perspectives are combined to explain something neither handles alone. |
| Clarify mechanism | The paper explains how and why a focal effect occurs. |
| Identify boundary conditions | The paper shows when, where, for whom, or under what conditions an effect changes. |
| Reconcile contradiction | The paper explains inconsistent prior findings or competing expectations. |
| Contextualize phenomenon | A new or changing business phenomenon is placed in a theory-facing frame. |

Weak contribution signals:

- "This enriches the literature" without saying what changed.
- "This is the first study..." without a theoretical implication.
- A contribution that is broader than the model.
- Practical implications detached from the actual mechanism.

## Theory Positioning

Require one primary theory conversation. Ask:

- Which literature is the paper entering?
- What does this literature explain well?
- What can it not yet explain?
- Why is the chosen context or phenomenon theoretically useful?
- Which constructs do the theoretical work, and which are only controls or context?

Use auxiliary theories only when they add explanatory leverage. Cut or demote theories that are named but not used downstream.

## Discussion Pattern

A JBR discussion should interpret rather than repeat. Use this order unless the manuscript calls for another structure:

1. Brief recap of the research problem and key findings.
2. Theoretical implications by contribution type.
3. Practical and/or social implications tied to findings.
4. Boundary conditions and limitations.
5. Future research agenda.
6. Short conclusion.

Calibrate:

- If the method is correlational, avoid causal certainty.
- If the mechanism is theorized but not directly measured, describe it as an implied or proposed mechanism.
- If a boundary condition is tested, explain why it matters for theory and decision-making.
